Over the six-week formalization campaign the project ran as a sequence of
long-lived orchestrating sessions, each directing a changing population of
delegated sub-agents. Measured only over the sessions collected in the
project's evidence store (a documented lower bound, since a June logging gap
and other coverage gaps remain, \S\ref{sec:discussion}), the campaign consumed
at least 15.1M output tokens, an API-equivalent cost of \$6{,}973, and 199.1h
of active engagement time under a 5-minute inter-event gap cap; the campaign
window is fixed at 2026-06-10 through the release day 2026-07-20, with later
evidence-collection work mechanically excluded. % CLM-002
This section describes the workflow mechanisms that structured that effort,
as reconstructed from six sessions that were independently event-coded
against a fixed vocabulary (Table~\ref{tab:event-codes}) rather than
summarized impressionistically; the six sessions are a purposive sample
chosen for reconstruction value, not a random or exhaustive one. % CLM-004

\subsection{Role separation and delegation}

From the earliest surviving session (2026-06-15--06-20, spanning the initial
local-only development period into the opening of the axiom-removal
campaign) the orchestrator was already constrained to a single rule: it does
not edit target-language files itself; every change is dispatched through a
sub-agent call. In that session a single opus-class orchestrator issued 107
dispatches across three roles --- 47 to a coder role, 45 to a prover role,
and 12 to a planning role that vets statements before implementation begins,
plus 3 read-only exploration calls --- and used its own tool budget only for
planning, gating, and version-control operations.
% CLAIM: CLM-007, EV-2076 (session 74aab39b)
The same separation held during the axiom-removal campaign, where the
orchestrator's 152 dispatches were checked against an independently invoked
adjudicator process rather than against the orchestrator's own judgment.
% CLAIM: CLM-007, EV-0925 (session 90fa24bb)

By the pre-publication refactoring campaign the role set had differentiated
further. One session in that campaign ran an orchestrator plus five named
teammates and, beneath one of those teammates, eight further dynamically
spawned sub-agents, giving five distinguishable roles in total: an
orchestrator/architect that plans work, judges merge gates, and executes
merges; a documentation-normalization coder; an implementation coder
carrying a full issue end-to-end across multiple pull requests; a read-only
scout role that checks an issue's stated assumptions against the current
code before any implementation starts; and two structurally independent
reviewer roles running in parallel --- one auditing module layering, the
other performing an exhaustive per-declaration comparison against the target
branch, itself spawning one single-purpose verification sub-agent per pull
request rather than reviewing the batch serially. % EV-1669 (session 7e6156bf)
The scout role paid for itself directly: it pre-corrected eight
issue/code mismatches before implementation began, in each case avoiding
either an unnecessary re-proof or an incorrect refactor plan. % EV-1669

\subsection{Statement freeze and independent verification}

The organizing discipline for statement integrity is that a merged
declaration's type is treated as frozen: a later pull request may extend a
proof but may not narrow, delete, or silently generalize the statement it
proves without that change surfacing at review. Two independent-review
mechanisms enforce this in practice. First, an adjudicator process
structurally separate from the implementing coder inspects each candidate
statement before merge. During the axiom-removal campaign this adjudicator
rejected an over-strong hypothesis-free statement of a derivative-continuity
lemma --- provably false for the excluded parameter range --- before the
missing hypotheses were added and the corrected statement merged; the same
mechanism later found a \texttt{sorry} placed under a proposition that was
outright false for unconstrained inputs, settled by exhibiting a numeric
counterexample (a magnitude-80 discrepancy) against the constrained case (a
near-zero residual), after which the statement was narrowed to the
constrained domain before the placeholder was removed. % EV-0925, leray-hopf#20, leray-hopf#27
Second, during the refactoring campaign the per-declaration byte-diff
reviewer caught an unannounced deletion of a previously public theorem
inside a large extraction refactor; the theorem was restored as a thin
wrapper and re-verified before merge. % EV-1669, leray-hopf#120

These catches sit in deliberate contrast with one release-blocking
postmortem case (\S\ref{sec:incidents}) in which a declaration's
type was never subjected to this kind of independent scrutiny before it
shipped. % INC-001
An earlier direct-proof campaign against that same declaration, during the
axiom-removal campaign, ended not with a soundness verdict on its statement
but with a ``consumer'' verdict --- a finding that nothing downstream
referenced the declaration, which redirected effort elsewhere within
minutes of a months-scale attack plan being adopted. No point in that
session's log shows the statement's truth being checked directly, and the
false generalization was caught only later, during a separate release-time
review pass. % EV-0925
We state this as a contrast in review coverage, not as a claim that
independent statement review would necessarily have caught the defect
earlier; the two cases differ in more ways than the presence of that gate
alone.

\subsection{Adversarial review and merge gates}

An adversarial review pass, run at high effort against every pull request as
a final soundness gate, is a third, independent check layered on top of the
adjudicator and the byte-diff reviewer. In the earliest session it caught a
sub-agent's own incorrect claim that a reduction step was mathematically
unsound, showing a valid averaging-based route existed after all. % EV-2076
The gate's plumbing needed its own iteration: in the refactoring campaign,
the merge monitor initially watched only formal review events on the pull
request and missed a review that had in fact been delivered as a top-level
comment, stalling a ready-to-merge change for roughly twenty minutes until
the human supervisor pointed out the discrepancy. The monitor was rewritten
to watch three channels at once --- a formal review event, a bot-authored
top-level comment, and an in-repository acknowledgment marker --- and no
repeat occurred across the ten pull requests that followed in the same
session. % EV-1669
A later transient non-response from the same reviewer (a thirty-minute
timeout against a typical few-minute turnaround) was resolved by
re-requesting review rather than by falling back to an alternate route.
% EV-1669
Separately, during the axiom-removal campaign the review tool itself was
found pinned to an unavailable model identifier, causing every review call
to fail outright until the identifier was corrected. % EV-0925

\subsection{Model and skill escalation}

Orchestrator model choice changed over the campaign: an opus-class model
drove the orchestrator in the two earliest coded sessions, while by the
refactoring campaign the orchestrator ran on a newer general-purpose model
with all five named teammates and their dynamically spawned sub-agents
assigned to a lighter model; an escalation to the heavier model was
considered at several explicit decision points in that session but never
exercised, i.e.\ the documented escalation threshold was not crossed even
though the option remained available throughout. % EV-1669
Within a single session, model assignment was also role-typed rather than
uniform: in the earliest session, 104 of 107 sub-agent dispatches used a
long-context variant of the heavier model for proof and implementation
work, while the three read-only exploration dispatches ran on the lightest
available model. % EV-2076
Escalation failures were rarer than tooling failures but did occur: an
automatic safety classifier hard-blocked a repository-creation and push
action toward the target organization even after the human supervisor had
approved it explicitly in conversation; the action was completed by the
human supervisor directly from a terminal, and the agent wired the
resulting remote afterward. This is recorded as friction at the
local-only-to-hosted transition, not as a case resolved by any escalation
path available to the agent itself. % EV-2076

\subsection{Worktree-based parallel dispatch and conflict handling}

Parallel dispatch across separate worktrees became the default execution
shape from the axiom-removal campaign onward, rising from a single
orchestrator issuing sequential sub-agent calls to, by the release
campaign, multiple independently named agents each owning a worktree and
running concurrently under one supervising session. % EV-0925, EV-0247
Some conflicts were designed out pre-emptively: a single shared
root-level import file was restricted to one coder at a time once a
concurrent-edit collision on it was foreseen, and a workspace-cleanup
tool's file-discovery scope was narrowed after an early run matched
vendored dependency repositories rather than only the project's own files.
% EV-2076
Others surfaced live and were caught procedurally rather than avoided: a
reviewer detected that its review target was being concurrently edited by
an unrelated import-cleanup task mid-review and re-verified the final diff
before passing it, rather than passing the version it had started
reviewing; % EV-1669
and, during the release campaign, a stale-liveness misjudgment led to a
second named agent being dispatched onto a worktree already owned by a
first agent that had in fact not died, so that both ran against the same
worktree until the duplication was noticed and the second dispatch was
stopped. % EV-0247

\subsection{Resource-aware orchestration}

Resource management is treated only briefly here; the incident that forced
it into an explicit, standing set of rules --- a memory-exhaustion cascade
during the release campaign, traced to accumulated helper-process chains
left behind by prior review tooling rather than to the Lean build itself
--- is analyzed as a case study in \S\ref{sec:incidents}. The rules now in
force (a single global build lock, a shared hard-linked build cache across
worktrees, a pre-build memory-headroom check, and mandatory cleanup of an
agent's own helper processes on task completion) did not exist in any
earlier coded session; they are a direct, incident-triggered addition to
the workflow rather than an anticipated design choice. % EV-0247

\subsection{Evolution across the campaign}

Table~\ref{tab:event-codes} summarizes event counts over the six sessions
that were independently coded against a fixed event vocabulary (event codes
for statement errors, resource exhaustion, worktree conflicts, escalation
failures, and successful recovery, applied uniformly across sessions); this
is not a census of the full campaign, only of the sampled sessions. % CLM-004
Read against the chronology, almost every standing rule described above
traces to a specific, dated failure rather than to upfront design: the
statement-freeze discipline hardened after the earliest WEAKEN catches; the
three-channel review monitor followed directly from one missed top-level
comment; the resource rules followed directly from the memory-exhaustion
cascade. The one discipline present from the very first coded session is
commit-time re-verification of on-disk state before trusting a sub-agent's
reported progress, adopted after repeated unrelated-cause process deaths
(network and idle-timeout failures, not logic errors) left partially
written files behind; every one of those deaths was caught and reverted
before commit in that session. % EV-2076
We do not treat this single early practice as sufficient on its own to
explain the zero \texttt{FALSE-SUCCESS} count in Table~\ref{tab:event-codes}
--- the later sessions layer independent adjudication, byte-diff review, and
adversarial review on top of it, and the coded sample is six sessions, not
the whole campaign --- but it is the one mechanism common to all of them,
and no coded session records a false success reaching a merge.
