\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Large language model agents can now sustain mathematical formalization
efforts that span weeks and more than a hundred pull requests, rather than single
proof-search episodes. This capability shifts the central reliability
question. A proof assistant's kernel already guarantees that a
\emph{proved} declaration is correct given its stated type, but kernel
checking is silent on several other questions that determine whether a
formalization campaign is trustworthy: whether a stated type is the
theorem the author -- human or agent -- actually intended; whether a
previously proved, still-true declaration silently disappeared during a
later refactor; whether a build or test genuinely succeeded, as opposed to
being reported successful over a stale or partially written disk state;
and whether the project's own record of what happened during development
is itself accurate. In a regime where declarations, reviews, and refactors
are produced continuously by many concurrently dispatched agent processes,
these statement-level, process-level, and record-level failure modes are
not corner cases to be dismissed once proof-checking is presumed reliable
-- they are the load-bearing risk that remains after it.

We report on a formalization campaign that ran under exactly this regime.
Over roughly six weeks (2026-06-10 through the 2026-07-20 release day),
the project consumed, on evidence collected so far, at least 15.1M output
tokens, an API-equivalent cost of \$6{,}972.83, and 199.1\,h of active
engagement time under a five-minute inter-event gap cap -- all documented
lower bounds, since coverage gaps in the session-log record remain.
% CLAIM: CLM-002
The campaign's central artifact is a kernel-only Lean~4~\cite{moura2021lean4}
formalization, built on mathlib~\cite{mathlib2020}, of existence of
Leray--Hopf weak solutions~\cite{leray1934,hopf1951} to the homogeneous,
incompressible Navier--Stokes equations, comprising two capstone existence
theorems -- one on the periodic 3-torus $\mathbb{T}^3$, one on
$\mathbb{R}^3$ -- whose \texttt{\#print axioms} output is exactly the
three standard Lean kernel axioms, with zero project-declared axioms and
no \texttt{sorryAx} on either theorem~\cite{lerayhopf2026repo}.
% CLAIM: CLM-001
To our knowledge, based on a literature and repository survey conducted in
July 2026 and summarized in the related-work discussion
below, no machine-checked existence theory for a nonlinear
fluid PDE has previously been reported in Lean, Coq, or Isabelle/HOL; we
state this as a bound on what our survey found, not as a proof of
non-existence.\footnote{Search terms, dates, and the nearest adjacent
results are recorded in the project's related-work notes and re-checked
immediately before submission.}
% CLAIM: CLM-006

The closest points of comparison span three literatures. Among
AI-authored Lean projects, TauCeti~\cite{tauceti2026}, an AI-authored
library incubated by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, pursues
breadth over a thirteen-area roadmap under a human-governed roadmap and a
named review rubric, but reports no accompanying paper, no incident-level
account of failure modes, and no session-level evidence trail; our
project instead pursues depth against a single frozen scope, with a
named-incident case study as a central artifact rather than an omission.
Among formalized analysis adjacent to fluid PDE, mathlib's
Gagliardo--Nirenberg--Sobolev inequality~\cite{vandoorn2024gns} and a Lean
formalization of De Giorgi--Nash--Moser elliptic regularity
theory~\cite{armstrong2026degiorginashmoser} establish, respectively, a
building-block inequality and a regularity theory, but neither is an
existence theorem for a time-dependent nonlinear PDE; the nearest
precedent by subject matter, an AI-assisted formalization of well-posedness
for the linear, kinetic Vlasov equation~\cite{miller2026vlasov}, is
single-author and single-agent and reports no multi-agent governance or
incident structure. Large-scale human-team formalizations such as the Odd
Order Theorem~\cite{gonthier2013oddorder} and the Liquid Tensor
Experiment~\cite{commelin2023abstraction} manage project-scale complexity
through a human team and a specification-driven blueprint; our setting
substitutes a single scientific owner governing agent-authored
implementation and independent agent review, with a frozen
formalization-scope document playing the blueprint's role. Finally, the
agentic and LLM theorem-proving literature -- proof-search reinforcement
learning~\cite{xin2025deepseekproverv2}, premise
retrieval~\cite{yang2023leandojo}, Olympiad-level proof
search~\cite{deepmind2025alphaproof}, and human-AI collaborative proof
environments~\cite{zhou2026leanatlas} -- reports session- or
benchmark-scale results rather than a project-scale development process
with recorded incidents and recovery; framing evidence on rising
autonomous task horizons~\cite{metr2025longtasks} and an external
empirical study of agentic pull requests~\cite{agenticprs2026security}
motivate the reliability question from outside formalization, but neither
instruments a single campaign to the depth reported here.

This paper makes three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Completion of a kernel-only Lean~4 and mathlib formalization of
  Leray--Hopf weak-solution existence on $\mathbb{T}^3$ and $\mathbb{R}^3$,
  carried out under an agent-orchestrated development regime over roughly
  six weeks.
\item A workflow design -- statement-first development, independent
  adversarial review, and machine-checked verification gates -- together
  with an evidence-backed account of what that design caught and what it
  missed, drawn from named incidents rather than from impressions.
\item An evidence methodology for AI-assisted research: escrow of session
  logs with a manifest, machine-checked claim-to-evidence verification,
  and cost accounting reconciled against actual billing records.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:formalization} defines the formalized notion of a weak
solution and states the two capstone theorems and their exact scope.
Section~\ref{sec:workflow} describes the agent-orchestrated development
workflow -- role separation, statement freeze, adversarial review, and
resource-aware orchestration -- as reconstructed from coded session logs.
Section~\ref{sec:incidents} presents four incidents in which a build or an
axiom check was insufficient on its own to guarantee the campaign was
proceeding soundly, and what mechanism outside the build closed the gap in
each case. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the resulting governance
picture and its limits, together with the limitations of this work, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
